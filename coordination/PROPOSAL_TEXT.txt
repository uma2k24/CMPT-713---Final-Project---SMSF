Natural Language Grounding for 3D Facial Geometry

1 Problem Statement
Natural-language interfaces can make 3D facial editing
more accessible by allowing users to describe desired
changes rather than manually adjusting geometric pa-
rameters. We frame this as atext-to-parameter ground-
ingtask: mapping a natural-language editing instruction
to a vector of continuous, normalized facial-geometry
parameters (e.g., eyebrow elevation, mouth width) that
can be rendered with a parametric 3D face model. In-
terpreting an instruction such as “raise the eyebrows
slightly, widen the smile, but keep the jaw unchanged”
requires more than identifying facial attributes: a model
must understand modification intensity, relationships be-
tween attributes, and the composition of multiple edits.
We investigate whether consistency-aware training
improves the robustness and generalization of language
models on this task, specifically:
1.Paraphrase consistency:semantically equivalent
instructions should produce similar parameter vec-
tors regardless of wording.
2.Compositional generalization:models should
correctly interpret unseen combinations of individ-
ually familiar attributes and modifiers.
We choose these aspects because standard supervised
fine-tuning treats each instruction independently, and
such models are known to rely on surface wording and to
fail on novel combinations (Keysers et al., 2020). In an
editing interface, these failures are immediately visible:
rephrasing the same request produces a different face.
The continuous output space also allows precise, per-
attribute error measurement. We therefore hypothesize
that adding an explicit paraphrase-consistency objective
to supervised fine-tuning will reduce parameter predic-
tion error on unfamiliar phrasings and unseen attribute
combinations.
Our contributions are (1) a dataset of facial editing
instructions paired with parameter vectors; (2) a con-
trolled comparison of a rule-based baseline, a supervised
model, and our consistency-aware variant; and (3) an
analysis of whether consistency training improves ac-
curacy on unfamiliar phrasings and unseen attribute
combinations.
2 Related Work
Jiang et al. (2021) proposed Talk-to-Edit, which usesnatural-language dialogue to make fine-grained changes
to facial images, but edits 2D images through a GAN
latent space rather than explicit geometric parameters.
Li et al. (2017) introduced FLAME, a parametric 3D
face model that represents shape, expression, and pose
with numerical parameters; however, these parameters
are learned statistically and do not correspond directly
to intuitive language.
Other studies have explored how well models gener-
alize to unfamiliar combinations of concepts. Keysers
et al. (2020) showed that standard sequence-to-sequence
models degrade sharply on novel combinations of fa-
miliar primitives, while Yin et al. (2023) showed that
consistency regularization improves compositional gen-
eralization in semantic parsing and machine translation.
Our project is neither a re-implementation nor a repli-
cation. We fine-tune pretrained T5 (Raffel et al., 2020)
and adapt the consistency-regularization idea of Yin
et al. (2023) from discrete sequence outputs to contin-
uous parameter regression, using paraphrase pairs as
equivalent inputs. To our knowledge, this has not been
explored for grounding language in 3D facial geometry.
Rather than generating realistic faces, we study how
accurately and consistently a language model can in-
terpret instructions and convert them into meaningful
numerical values.

4 Experimental Setup
Data.As no existing dataset pairs editing instructions
with continuous 3D facial parameters, we construct our
own in three stages. (1) We generate ∼1,000 instruc-
tions from a template grammar over attributes, direc-
tions, intensity modifiers, preservation cues, and multi-
edit conjunctions; gold vectors yare derived composi-
tionally, yielding exact labels. (2) Each instruction is
paraphrased using an LLM and manually verified for
meaning preservation, producing pairs (x, x′). (3) We
collect 150–200 instructions written by participants un-
familiar with our templates, which both authors annotate
separately using the same fixed intensity scale as the
templates, resolving disagreements by discussion. Para-
phrases of the same instruction never appear in different
splits (Table 2).

Implementation.We use PyTorch and Hugging Face
Transformers with pretrained T5-base weights, and im-
plement the data generator, rule-based baseline, output
heads, consistency loss, and evaluation ourselves. If
time permits, we will integrate predicted parameters
with a parametric 3D face model such as FLAME for
visualization. Since FLAME’s parameters do not corre-
spond directly to our attributes, this requires manually
defining how each attribute maps to its geometry.Evaluation.We report six metrics.Attribute F1
measures whether the model identifies the modified at-
tributes.MAEmeasures the error between predicted
and gold values.Paraphrase consistencyis the mean
Lcons(Eq. 2) over test paraphrase pairs.Compositional
accuracyis the fraction of Test-Comp instructions with
all values within ϵ= 0.1 of gold.Negation accuracy
is the fraction of preserved attributes predicted within
ϵof0.Intensity accuracyis the fraction of modifier
pairs (e.g., “slightly” vs. “much”) ordered correctly by
magnitude. Because a constant predictor is trivially con-
sistent, we always report consistency alongside MAE.
Formal definitions are given in Appendix A.
Comparison.Comparing A with B measures the ben-
efit of learned representations, while comparing B with
C, trained on identical data, isolates the effect of the
consistency objective. We vary λ∈ {0.1,0.5,1.0} and
report mean ±std over three seeds. Since a constant
predictor is trivially consistent, we judge Model C by
whether it reduces error on Test-Comp and Test-Human,
not by consistency alone.
